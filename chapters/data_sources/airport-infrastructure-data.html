28  Airport and infrastructure data

Author

Xavier Olive

Airport infrastructure data complement trajectories, flight plans, weather reports, and operational records. They tell us where an aircraft can land, taxi, park, board passengers, load cargo, or be constrained by local procedures. At the simplest level, an airport is a coded point on a map. At the most detailed level, it is a time-dependent surface model made of runways, taxiways, aprons, stands, buildings, service roads, obstacles, and declared operational limits.

28.1 Airport identifiers

For most aviation datasets, only two airport identifiers are routinely useful: the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) location indicator and the IATA airport code.

An ICAO location indicator is a four-letter code such as LFPG for Paris Charles de Gaulle or EHAM for Amsterdam Schiphol. It is the preferred identifier in air traffic management, flight plans, meteorological messages, aeronautical information, and many operational datasets. It is also the safest code to use when joining airport data with trajectories or flight-intent data.

An IATA airport code is a three-letter code such as CDG or AMS. It is common in airline schedules, ticketing, passenger data, and public-facing datasets. IATA codes are convenient, but they do not cover all aerodromes and are less natural for air traffic control data.

TipICAO or IATA?

Use ICAO codes when working with flight plans, surveillance data, METAR/TAF reports, AIP material, or air traffic management data. Use IATA codes when working with airline schedules, passenger demand, booking data, or public airport datasets. Many analyses need a lookup table between the two.

28.2 Airport reference points

Most airport-level datasets describe each airport with a small set of attributes: name, ICAO and IATA codes when available, country, municipality, coordinates, elevation, and a broad type or status. The coordinates usually represent an airport reference point: a conventional point associated with the aerodrome as a whole. It should not be interpreted as a runway threshold, a terminal centroid, or the point where aircraft enter or leave the surface network.

This coarse representation is nevertheless useful. Airport reference points are enough to compute approximate great-circle distances between origin and destination airports, to provide simple models with a rough estimate of remaining flight distance, and to attach a first-order airport elevation to vertical profiles. They are also convenient for global maps and for joining flight plans or schedules to airport metadata.

The limitation is that a single point cannot describe how an airport is used operationally. Runway assignment, taxi distance, stand allocation, surface movement, runway occupancy, and local constraints require more detailed infrastructure layers. For trajectory analysis, airport reference points are therefore usually the first enrichment layer: a flight with origin EHAM and destination LFPG can be joined to airport names, coordinates, countries, and approximate distances before more detailed runway or taxiway information is considered.

28.3 Runway data

Runways are the first infrastructure layer below the airport reference point. A runway dataset usually contains one record per physical runway or per runway direction, with fields such as designator, threshold coordinates, length, width, surface, lighting, elevation, displaced threshold, and sometimes declared distances. These fields are essential when the analysis depends on how aircraft actually use the airport rather than simply which airport they visit.

Runway data are used to infer landing or departure runways from trajectory geometry, estimate active runway configurations, compare runway use with wind or noise constraints, approximate take-off and landing performance, and link safety events to the relevant runway environment.

For many trajectory analyses, runway thresholds are the first geometric features that matter: they provide an operational reference that is more precise than the airport reference point but still much simpler than a full surface-movement model. They are also a useful proxy for approach analysis: because precision approaches such as the Instrument Landing System guide aircraft along a localizer aligned with the runway axis, threshold coordinates and runway bearings help detect whether a trajectory is established on final approach, which runway direction it is using, and where deviations from the expected path begin.

NoteRunway names are not stable identifiers

Runway designators are based on magnetic bearing, rounded to the nearest ten degrees and written with two digits. As magnetic variation changes, the painted runway number may have to change even though the pavement, thresholds, and geometry remain the same.

The examples below show what matters for data work: a redesignation may affect one runway or an entire runway system; the effective date may be precise to the day or only known from a works period; and not all changes have the same cause. Most examples follow magnetic drift, but Helsinki shows another common case: adding a parallel runway changed the suffixes of an existing runway.

28.4 Surface infrastructure data

Taxiways, aprons, stands, gates, holding positions, terminals, service roads, and airport boundaries are more difficult to model than airports and runways. They are geometric objects with local naming conventions and variable data quality. In open data, they are often best represented in OpenStreetMap using aeroway=* tags (documentation). In authoritative data, they appear in AIP aerodrome charts, airport GIS systems, or commercial aeronautical databases.

  const runwayCoordinates = (feature) => {
    const geometry = feature.geometry;
    if (geometry.type === "LineString") return geometry.coordinates;
    if (geometry.type === "Polygon") {
      const ring = geometry.coordinates[0] ?? [];
      let best = [ring[0], ring.at(-1)];
      let bestDistance = -1;
      for (let i = 0; i < ring.length; i += 1) {
        for (let j = i + 1; j < ring.length; j += 1) {
          const distance = (ring[i][0] - ring[j][0]) ** 2 + (ring[i][1] - ring[j][1]) ** 2;
          if (distance > bestDistance) {
            bestDistance = distance;
            best = [ring[i], ring[j]];
          }
        }
      }
      return best;
    }
    return [];
  };
  const runwayLabelAngle = (from, to) => {
    const meanLatitude = ((from[1] + to[1]) / 2) * Math.PI / 180;
    const dx = (to[0] - from[0]) * Math.cos(meanLatitude);
    const dy = to[1] - from[1];
    return Math.atan2(-dy, dx) * 180 / Math.PI + 90;
  };
  const runwayBearing = (from, to) => {
    const meanLatitude = ((from[1] + to[1]) / 2) * Math.PI / 180;
    const dx = (to[0] - from[0]) * Math.cos(meanLatitude);
    const dy = to[1] - from[1];
    return (Math.atan2(dx, dy) * 180 / Math.PI + 360) % 360;
  };
  const runwayLabelPosition = (threshold, opposite) => {
    const offset = 0.045;
    return [
      threshold[0] + (threshold[0] - opposite[0]) * offset,
      threshold[1] + (threshold[1] - opposite[1]) * offset

    .filter((designator) => designator !== excluded)

  };
  const runwayLabelCandidates = runwayFeatures.features.flatMap((feature) => {
    const designators = runwayDesignators(feature.properties.ref);
    const coordinates = runwayCoordinates(feature).filter(Boolean);
    if (designators.length < 2 || coordinates.length < 2) return [];
    const first = coordinates[0];
    const last = coordinates.at(-1);
    const firstDesignator = bestRunwayDesignator(designators, runwayBearing(first, last));
    const lastDesignator = bestRunwayDesignator(designators, runwayBearing(last, first), firstDesignator);
    if (!firstDesignator || !lastDesignator) return [];
    const firstLabel = runwayLabelPosition(first, last);
    const lastLabel = runwayLabelPosition(last, first);
    return [
      { longitude: firstLabel[0], latitude: firstLabel[1], label: firstDesignator, angle: runwayLabelAngle(first, last) },
      { longitude: lastLabel[0], latitude: lastLabel[1], label: lastDesignator, angle: runwayLabelAngle(last, first) }
    ];
  });
  const runwayLabels = Array.from(
    d3.group(runwayLabelCandidates, (candidate) => candidate.label),
    ([, candidates]) => candidates
      .map((candidate) => ({ ...candidate, projection: runwayLabelProjection(candidate) }))
      .sort((a, b) => d3.ascending(a.projection, b.projection))[0]
  );
  const plotDomain = {
    type: "FeatureCollection",
    features: [
      ...airportGeoJSON.features,
      ...runwayLabels.map((label) => ({
        type: "Feature",
        properties: { label: label.label },
        geometry: { type: "Point", coordinates: [label.longitude, label.latitude] }
      }))
    ]
  };
  const fillOpacity = (feature) => {
    const category = feature.properties.aeroway;
    if (category === "terminal") return 0.65;
    if (category === "apron") return 0.25;
    return 0;
  };

  const plot = Plot.plot({
    width,
    height,
    className: "airport-svg",
    color: {
      domain: Array.from(aerowayColors.keys()),
      range: Array.from(aerowayColors.values()),
      legend: true
    },
    projection: {
      type: "mercator",
      domain: plotDomain,
      inset: 55
    },
    marks: [
      Plot.geo(nonRunwayFeatures, {
        stroke: (feature) => feature.properties.aeroway,
        fill: (feature) => feature.properties.aeroway,
        strokeWidth: (feature) => feature.properties.aeroway === "taxiway" ? 2 : 1,
        r: 1,
        fillOpacity,
        title
      }),
      Plot.geo(runwayFeatures, {
        stroke: () => "runway",
        fill: () => "runway",
        strokeWidth: 5,
        r: 2,
        fillOpacity: 0,
        title
      }),
      Plot.text(runwayLabels, {
        x: "longitude",
        y: "latitude",
        text: "label",
        rotate: "angle",
        fontSize: 12,
        fontWeight: 400,
        fill: "#111827",
        halo: "white",
        haloWidth: 3
      })
    ]
  });

  const filenameBase = `${selectedAirport?.icao ?? "airport"}-surface`.toLowerCase();
  const plotSvgNode = () => {
    if (plot instanceof SVGSVGElement) return plot;
    const svgs = Array.from(plot.querySelectorAll("svg"));
    return svgs
      .map((svg) => ({
        svg,
        area: (Number(svg.getAttribute("width")) || svg.clientWidth || 0)
          * (Number(svg.getAttribute("height")) || svg.clientHeight || 0)
      }))
      .sort((a, b) => d3.descending(a.area, b.area))[0]?.svg;
  };
  const serializeSvg = () => {
    const svgNode = plotSvgNode();
    const clone = svgNode.cloneNode(true);
    clone.setAttribute("xmlns", "http://www.w3.org/2000/svg");
    clone.setAttribute("width", width);
    clone.setAttribute("height", height);
    clone.setAttribute("viewBox", clone.getAttribute("viewBox") ?? `0 0 ${width} ${height}`);
    return new XMLSerializer().serializeToString(clone);
  };
  const svg = serializeSvg();
  const svgHref = `data:image/svg+xml;charset=utf-8,${encodeURIComponent(svg)}`;
  const pngLink = htl.html`<a download=${`${filenameBase}.png`} href="#" aria-disabled="true">Preparing PNG…</a>`;

  const image = new Image();
  image.onload = () => {
    const scale = 2;
    const canvas = document.createElement("canvas");
    canvas.width = width * scale;
    canvas.height = height * scale;
    const context = canvas.getContext("2d");
    context.fillStyle = "#ffffff";
    context.fillRect(0, 0, canvas.width, canvas.height);
    context.drawImage(image, 0, 0, canvas.width, canvas.height);
    pngLink.href = canvas.toDataURL("image/png");
    pngLink.textContent = "Download PNG";
    pngLink.removeAttribute("aria-disabled");
  };
  image.onerror = () => {
    pngLink.textContent = "PNG unavailable";
    pngLink.setAttribute("aria-disabled", "true");
  };
  image.src = svgHref;

  return htl.html`<div>
    ${plot}
    <div class="airport-download-buttons">
      <a download=${`${filenameBase}.svg`} href=${svgHref}>Download SVG</a>
      ${pngLink}
    </div>
  </div>`;
}

28.5 Main open data sources

28.5.1 OurAirports

OurAirports provides global CSV files for airports, runways, navaids, countries, and regions. It is simple to download, easy to parse, and useful for examples, exploratory work, and global airport maps. Its main limitation is that it is community-maintained rather than authoritative. Completeness varies by country and by airport size, and detailed operational fields such as declared distances may be absent or incomplete.

28.5.2 Aeronautical Information Publications

The authoritative source for airport infrastructure is usually the AIP AD section published by a state or by its aeronautical information service. AIPs include aerodrome charts, runway dimensions, declared distances, lighting, local procedures, operating hours, rescue and firefighting categories, obstacles, and many restrictions. They are authoritative, but not always easy to use as data: access is fragmented across national portals, formats vary, and much information is provided as PDF charts or semi-structured HTML.

28.5.3 OpenStreetMap

OpenStreetMap is often the most accessible source for detailed surface geometry: airport boundaries, runways, taxiways, aprons, terminals, gates, stands, and service roads. It is particularly useful for visualisation, map matching, and prototyping surface-movement analyses. The trade-off is that OSM is not an aeronautical publication. Tags may be inconsistent, topology may be incomplete, and updates are not tied to the AIRAC cycle.

28.5.4 National and airport-specific sources

Some countries publish airport registries, runway datasets, obstacle databases, or open GIS layers. Some airport operators publish open-data portals with terminal, stand, capacity, or operational information. These sources can be excellent for local studies but rarely scale globally.

28.6 Data quality and versioning

Airport infrastructure is not static. Runways are closed, extended, resurfaced, or renumbered; taxiway systems are rebuilt; stands change aircraft compatibility; terminals open and close; local restrictions evolve. Authoritative aeronautical data are managed through Aeronautical Information Regulation And Control (AIRAC) cycles, while open geographic data are updated continuously and unevenly.

For reproducible analyses, record the source, extraction date, and version whenever possible. For operational or safety-critical work, use authoritative aeronautical information rather than crowdsourced geometry. For exploratory analysis and visualisation, combining OurAirports metadata with OpenStreetMap geometry is often a practical starting point.
