35  Flight plans, intent, and Network Manager trajectories

Authors

Enrico Spinielli

Xavier Olive

A flight plan is a declaration of intent: where an aircraft is expected to depart, where it is expected to arrive, which route it requests, which level it requests, and which aircraft and equipment will be used. It is not the same as the flown trajectory. Between filing and landing, the route may be amended by flow-management measures, controller clearances, weather deviations, or tactical decisions.

This chapter focuses on flight-plan and trajectory-intent data sources. The operational role of flight plans in air traffic services and flow management is introduced in air traffic services, separation, and flow management. The published points, routes, airspaces, and procedures that flight plans refer to are introduced in aeronautical information and data objects.

35.1 Intent layers

There is rarely a single definitive “planned trajectory”. Analysts often need to distinguish several layers:

Layer Meaning Typical source
filed intent what the aircraft operator files before departure ICAO FPL, airline systems, IFPS/NM data
accepted or regulated intent what the network/flow system accepts after validation and restrictions Network Manager systems, ATFM messages
tactical intent clearances and amendments during operation CPDLC, voice-derived records, controller systems
computed trajectory trajectory model derived from flight plan, performance, restrictions, and environment NM trajectory models, planning systems
flown trajectory what surveillance or ADS-C reports show actually happened ADS-B, radar, ADS-C, MLAT

A good analysis keeps these layers separate. A filed route string is not a trajectory; a modeled trajectory is not surveillance truth; a flown trajectory does not reveal the original clearance unless joined with communication or flight-plan data.

35.2 Flight-plan content

An ICAO flight plan contains structured fields for flight identification, rules, type of flight, aircraft type, wake turbulence category, equipment, departure aerodrome, departure time, cruising speed, level, route, destination, alternate aerodromes, endurance, persons on board, and other operational information.

For trajectory analysis, the most important fields are usually:

  • aircraft identification or callsign;
  • aircraft type and performance-relevant information;
  • departure and destination aerodromes;
  • estimated off-block or departure time;
  • requested speed and cruising level;
  • route field;
  • equipment and capability indicators;
  • alternates and remarks when relevant.

The route field is a compact textual representation of intent. It may combine procedure names, waypoints, ATS routes, direct segments, speed/level changes, and special indicators. It must be interpreted against the aeronautical information cycle effective for the flight date.

Example route fragment:

N0441F340 FISTO5B FISTO UY156 PERIG UT210 TUDRA/N0426F280 UT158 AMB AMB9W

This is not a sequence of coordinates. It is a route expression that must be expanded through the relevant procedure and route definitions.

35.3 From filed route to trajectory

A route string can be expanded into a sequence of published points and segments, but a 4D trajectory requires more information:

  • aircraft performance model;
  • expected mass and speed schedule;
  • wind and temperature assumptions;
  • altitude constraints and level changes;
  • route restrictions and availability;
  • airport and runway assumptions;
  • flow-management measures.

This is why systems such as EUROCONTROL’s Network Manager maintain trajectory models rather than only storing the submitted flight plan. The model is updated as new information becomes available.

35.4 Network Manager context

EUROCONTROL’s Network Manager (NM) supports flight-plan validation, demand prediction, capacity balancing, and flow management in the European network. Aircraft operators filing IFR flights in the NM area submit flight-plan information that is checked against route availability, restrictions, sector capacities, and other operational constraints.

The result is a set of trajectory and demand data products used by ANSPs, airports, aircraft operators, researchers, and tools such as NEST. Access may be operational, institutional, or subject to EUROCONTROL data-sharing conditions.

35.5 NM trajectory models

Network Manager trajectory products distinguish several flight trajectory models. The names below are commonly encountered in DDR2/NM contexts.

35.5.1 Model 1: FTFM

Model 1, or Filed Tactical Flight Model (FTFM), is the trajectory model initially created from the filed flight plan and subsequent pre-tactical updates. It translates the route, especially the route field, into a point and airspace profile.

The model can include:

  • a sequence of 4D positions;
  • penetrated airspaces and sectors;
  • estimated times;
  • requested or computed levels;
  • aircraft type and performance assumptions;
  • route restrictions, including the Route Availability Document (RAD).

FTFM is therefore a computed intent trajectory: it is more structured than the filed route string, but it is still a plan.

35.5.2 Model 2: RTFM

Model 2, or Regulated Tactical Flight Model (RTFM), reflects the trajectory after regulation. A regulation may impose delay or require a rerouting to balance demand and capacity. The update can be more than a simple time shift because route availability, airspace restrictions, or sector configurations may differ in the new time window.

35.5.3 Model 3: CTFM

Model 3, or Current Tactical Flight Model (CTFM), updates the trajectory during operation. It may incorporate correlated position reports, radar-derived data, ADS-B reports, and other updates when the aircraft deviates from earlier models.

CTFM is closer to the flown trajectory than FTFM or RTFM, but it is still a model maintained by a network system. It should not be treated as identical to raw surveillance data.

35.6 SO6 segment files

SO6 is a segment-based format. Instead of representing a trajectory only as points, it stores flight segments from origin to destination. A typical segment contains the two endpoint names, origin and destination airports, aircraft type, begin and end times, begin and end flight levels, segment status, callsign, dates, endpoint coordinates, flight identifier, sequence number, segment length, and flight-level parity.

Field group Examples
flight identifiers origin, destination, aircraft type, callsign, flight identifier
segment timing begin/end date and time
vertical profile begin/end flight level, climb/descent/cruise status
geometry begin/end point names, begin/end coordinates, segment length
sequencing segment sequence number, parity/allocation flags

SO6 is useful for airspace-demand and route-usage analysis because each segment can be intersected with sectors, airspaces, or route structures. Its segment representation is less natural for high-rate trajectory processing than point-by-point surveillance data, but more compact for network demand analysis.

35.7 ALL_FT+

ALL_FT+ is a large Network Manager archive format available through DDR2. Files are typically named by date, for example yyyyMMdd.ALL_FT+, and may contain hundreds of columns depending on version. It is best understood as an internal operational archive exposed for analysis rather than as a clean public data model.

Typical information includes:

  • flight identifiers and callsign;
  • departure and destination aerodromes;
  • aircraft type;
  • flight-plan and regulation fields;
  • trajectory point sequence;
  • timestamps and positions;
  • flight levels and speeds;
  • airspace and sector penetrations;
  • route and restriction information.

The richness is attractive, but documentation and metadata can be sparse. Analysts should build small, explicit extraction layers rather than relying on column positions without version checks.

35.8 DDR2 demand files

EUROCONTROL’s Demand Data Repository (DDR2) provides historical demand datasets for selected periods and areas. A common demand file, often referred to as exp2, contains one row per flight with fields such as departure time, origin and destination aerodromes, unique flight identifier, callsign, aircraft type, and requested flight level.

Such files are useful for aggregate demand studies, sector-load estimation, traffic-flow analysis, and comparisons between planned and flown traffic. They usually need to be joined with aeronautical information, sector definitions, and surveillance trajectories to answer detailed operational questions.

35.9 Planned versus flown trajectories

Planned and flown trajectories differ for several reasons:

  • tactical shortcuts or directs;
  • weather deviations;
  • departure delay or slot regulation;
  • runway or procedure changes;
  • level-off during climb;
  • flow-management rerouting;
  • holding, vectoring, or sequencing near arrival;
  • aircraft performance and wind differences.

A robust analysis should name which comparison is being made: filed route versus flown path, FTFM versus ADS-B, RTFM versus CTFM, or NM demand versus actual traffic. Each comparison answers a different question.

TipUseful joins

Flight-plan and NM data become more useful when joined with other sources: ADS-B or radar trajectories for actual movement, ADS-C for oceanic position and intent, CPDLC for clearances, AIRAC-cycle aeronautical data for route expansion, and weather data for rerouting or performance analysis.

35.10 Access and quality caveats

Flight-plan and NM trajectory data are operational data. Access is often restricted, licensed, or subject to aggregation rules. Even when available, the data requires careful handling:

  • route strings depend on the correct AIRAC cycle;
  • identifiers can change between filing, operation, and surveillance;
  • timestamps may represent planned, calculated, regulated, or observed times;
  • modeled trajectories depend on assumptions not always visible in the file;
  • late flight-plan changes can invalidate earlier intent;
  • surveillance-derived updates can blur the distinction between plan and flown path.

Keep the source, model name, cycle, timestamp semantics, and flight identifier lineage explicit. Without these, intent data can easily be mistaken for observed truth.
