29  Flight tracking and surveillance data

Authors

Xavier Olive

Martin Strohmeier

Flight tracking datasets describe how an aircraft state evolves over time.

In their most common tabular form, they contain timestamped positions, altitudes, speeds, vertical rates, and identifiers such as the aircraft address or callsign. They may come from aircraft recorders, Air Navigation Service Provider (ANSP) systems, crowdsourced radio receivers, commercial aggregators, or specialised low-level traffic networks.

This page focuses on data sources: what they contain, how they are accessed, and what limitations matter for analysis. The surveillance technologies themselves are introduced in the background section; raw decoding of ADS-B data is treated in the next chapter.

29.1 What is a trajectory dataset?

A trajectory is a mathematical abstraction used to describe the evolution of a moving object with a finite list of parameters. In aviation, the usual state vector includes latitude, longitude, altitude, and timestamp, often completed with first derivatives such as ground speed, track angle, and vertical rate. Depending on the application, additional variables may be required: aircraft performance models, for example, may use pitch, roll, yaw, true airspeed, indicated airspeed, Mach number, or aircraft mass estimates.

We usually distinguish three levels of data:

  • raw information, such as Mode S or ADS-B frames received by a radio receiver, radar plots, or ASTERIX records before they have been normalised into a common table;
  • decoded states, where message or sensor contents have been converted into physical values such as position, callsign, altitude, velocity, or squawk code;
  • reconstructed flights, where points have been grouped, cleaned, interpolated, and enriched into coherent trajectories.

Most analysis starts from the second or third level. The first level is essential when studying decoding, receiver networks, message quality, or protocol details.

29.2 Sources of trajectory data

29.2.1 Flight data recorders and quick access recorders

The most comprehensive trajectory and aircraft-state data are produced by the aircraft itself, especially by the Flight Data Recorder (FDR) and Quick Access Recorder (QAR). These records may contain thousands of parameters and support Flight Operational Quality Assurance (FOQA), post-operations safety analysis, operational-efficiency studies, and predictive maintenance.

Recorder data are usually owned by the aircraft operator and are highly sensitive. They can expose operational practices, aircraft handling, maintenance information, and commercial strategies. Researchers may access such data only under strict agreements, and these data cannot support open global analyses across all aircraft in a region.

29.2.2 ANSP radar and surveillance tracks

Air navigation service providers build surveillance tracks from systems such as radar, Mode S, ADS-B, and multilateration. The data product available to controllers is not usually a raw sensor feed but a processed traffic picture: plots are associated to tracks, filtered, smoothed, fused, and sometimes correlated with flight plans.

A typical radar or fused surveillance track contains an identifier, timestamps, position, altitude, ground speed, vertical speed, and track angle. Kalman filters and other tracking algorithms may smooth positions and compute derivatives.

Important

ANSPs own the data produced by the surveillance installations they operate. Systematic access to radar or fused surveillance trajectories is therefore difficult, and international flights may require agreements with several providers.

Radar and fused tracks have excellent operational value, but they are rarely available as open data. When available, they may be provided in operational exchange formats such as ASTERIX, in post-processed archives, or through research agreements. Access conditions should be checked early: these records are operational data, receiver and sensor locations may be sensitive, and redistribution is often restricted.

ASTERIX is a family of category-specific formats rather than a single trajectory schema:

  • CAT048 is used for monoradar target reports. It may contain the data source identifier, time of day, measured polar position, Mode A squawk, flight level, aircraft address, aircraft identification, Mode S Comm-B payloads, track number, calculated position, and calculated track velocity. Positions may be radar-local polar or Cartesian quantities, so conversion to latitude/longitude depends on the radar reference and processing chain.
  • CAT021 is used for ADS-B target reports. It commonly carries WGS-84 position, aircraft address, time of day, flight level, ground vector, target identification, Mode 3/A code, and quality indicators; however, CAT021 is version-sensitive, so the same category number can map field-reference numbers to different data items across editions.

29.2.3 Crowdsourced and open receiver networks

Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) and Mode S data are widely collected by networks of ground receivers. Public websites such as Flightradar24, FlightAware aggregate messages from distributed receivers and produce aircraft state vectors or flight tracks. Access ranges from open research datasets to commercial APIs; licensing, privacy filtering, and redistribution rules differ by provider. The OpenSky Network provide an open access to historical data for academics, limited to research usage.

ADS-B is especially important because aircraft broadcast their own position, altitude, velocity, and identification without being interrogated. Mode S replies and Comm-B messages may add identity, altitude, aircraft parameters, or intent-related fields when interrogated by compatible ground infrastructure. Raw decoding details belong in Decode ADS-B and Mode S data.

Ground receiver coverage is dense in many populated regions and sparse over oceans, polar areas, deserts, and politically sensitive regions. Satellite ADS-B reduces some of these gaps by receiving 1090 MHz broadcasts from low-Earth-orbit satellites, but access is usually commercial or institutional.

29.2.4 Contract-based surveillance and datalink sources

Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Contract (ADS-C) can also provide trajectory data, especially in oceanic and remote airspace. Unlike ADS-B, it is not an open broadcast. It is based on a contract between an aircraft and an Air Traffic Service Unit (ATSU) specifying what information should be sent and under which conditions.

For flight tracking, ADS-C reports are valuable because they may include not only a position but also route intent, meteorological information, and air-reference data. These messages have traditionally been available mainly to operational stakeholders, but ADS-C data have also started to appear in research-oriented feeds, including the OpenSky Network. The detailed data model and decoded examples are covered in ADS-C data. Datalink context is introduced in Datalink and ACARS message sources.

29.2.5 Low-level and general aviation tracking

Not all flight tracking relies on certified transponders and 1090 MHz ADS-B. Low-level, general aviation, glider, and emerging uncrewed-aircraft operations use additional systems.

Universal Access Transceiver (UAT) is an ADS-B-like technology operating on 978 MHz. In the United States, it is used mainly by general aviation aircraft below 18,000 feet as an alternative to 1090 MHz Extended Squitter. It is therefore geographically and operationally limited: UAT data are mainly relevant within or near US airspace.

FLARM is widely used for traffic awareness and collision avoidance in gliders and other light aircraft. It broadcasts position, altitude, velocity, aircraft type, and short-term trajectory-prediction information. Its radio protocol is proprietary and encrypted, but crowdsourced reception is possible through networks such as the Open Glider Network. The OpenSky Network also collects FLARM raw messages for institutional research users.

Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Light (ADS-L) is a developing cooperative surveillance technology for low-level airspace, typically below 10,000 feet. It targets aircraft and devices that may not carry conventional ADS-B equipment: gliders, light sport aircraft, paragliders, helicopters, parachuting operations, and Unmanned Aircraft System (UAS). It is still undergoing validation and regulatory development, so operational deployment and data access remain limited.

29.3 An example trajectory

Even when all fields are not available at each timestamp, a tabular representation is the common starting point. Typical columns include a timestamp, aircraft icao24 address, callsign, position, barometric or geometric altitude, ground speed, track, vertical rate, squawk, and sometimes receiver or source metadata. In this example, the icao24 code 7c4779 matches a Qantas Boeing 747 registered as VH-OEJ.

TipWhat is a callsign

A callsign is the identifier used in communication with ATC. General aviation often uses the aircraft registration, while commercial flights commonly use an airline telephony designator and a flight number, such as BAW (pronounced “speedbird”) for British Airways or AFR for Air France. Other operations may encode a mission in the callsign: calibration flights, firefighting, medical helicopters, test flights, or military operations.

For data analysis, callsigns are useful but fragile. They are not globally unique, may be missing, can persist after a flight changes phase, and may be reused by different aircraft at different times. They should usually be combined with the aircraft address, time, and route context.

29.4 Data quality and coverage

Flight tracking data are not a neutral record of all aircraft movements: they reflect the surveillance technology, receiver deployment, aircraft equipage, regulatory mandates, filtering policies, and access conditions behind each feed.

  • Coverage and sampling are the first limitations to inspect. Ground receivers are unevenly distributed, low-altitude aircraft disappear behind terrain or the radio horizon, satellite ADS-B has its own reception constraints, and ADS-B, radar, ADS-C, FLARM, UAT, and ADS-L all operate at different update rates; these differences affect event timing, speed estimates, vertical-rate estimates, phase detection, and comparisons between datasets.

  • Timing is also source-dependent: ADS-B and Mode S messages do not carry a full emission timestamp, receiver clocks may drift, and network aggregation may replace local reception times with later processing times.

  • Position and altitude fields require similar caution. ADS-B positions are aircraft-reported and depend on the onboard navigation solution, while radar and MLAT derive positions externally; fused tracks may hide which sensor contributed a given point.

  • Barometric altitude, flight level, and geometric altitude do not describe the same quantity, and ISA/QNH assumptions or airport elevation become important near the surface.

  • Several variables that look like direct measurements may in fact be derived, smoothed, or interpolated by the receiver or network, including ground speed, track angle, and vertical rate

  • Identity and continuity are another source of error: callsigns may be stale or missing, aircraft addresses can be duplicated, reassigned, anonymised, or filtered, and long gaps may fragment a single flight into several tracks.

These issues matter before modelling traffic flows, estimating performance, measuring emissions, or comparing flown trajectories with planned routes. Raw or decoded points therefore usually need preprocessing before they become analytically useful flights: grouping by aircraft and callsign, splitting flights with time gaps or airport context, removing impossible jumps and duplicates, resampling, detecting flight phases, and enriching the trajectory with aircraft, airport, weather, airspace, or flight-plan data.

The appropriate strategy depends on the question: a surface-movement study near an airport needs different assumptions from a continent-scale flow analysis or an aircraft-performance estimation. For a broader discussion of trajectory preprocessing challenges and reproducibility, see [1].
